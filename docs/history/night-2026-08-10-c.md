## 3. The FOUR instrument defects

None of them is the agent's. All four make a published number mean something
other than it appeared to — and the fourth took the night's headline with it.

### 3.0 The oracle scored one body's prediction against another

**The most expensive one, and it was found the next day by reading the code with
the boards in front of us.**

`judge()` took `plan.remain_hp` and compared it with whichever single opposing
body had lost hit points that step. The plan carries `target` — 0 for their
active, 1 and up for their bench — and **nothing read it**.

Of the **611 dumped `PHANTOM_KO`, 545 (89.2%)** pointed at a body on their
**bench** while the attack landed on the active. The agent was saying *if I gust
that, it falls*, and it was right; then it did not gust, it attacked the wall,
and the wall survived.

That is also **why Crustle topped every table**: against a wall the best prize
route is a gust to the bench, so that is where the plan points and that is where
the misattribution lives. The concentration belonged to the detector.

| `crustle_wall_9`, n=1 000 | before | now |
|---|---:|---:|
| `PHANTOM_KO` | 124 | **17** |
| `DAMAGE_DRIFT` | 76 | 42 |
| rate for the list | 4.92% | **1.58%** |

Fixed (`planned_serial`), pinned in eleven tests and validated in both
directions: a phantom **on the plan's own body** is still reported, and what is
discarded is **counted** in the report instead of disappearing.

What survives is what was correctly attributed: the engine resolves the base
damage, and it is not the stadium, not a tool, not the double attack. What does
not survive is the magnitude — nor the urgency. §5 of the deep dive already
warned that the residue did not explain the matchup (r = +0.09); now we know
why.

### 3.1 The permutation probe was measuring a mirror

`over_games` called `battle_start(deck, deck)`: **both seats were our own deck**.
The 0.63–0.67% that three night plans cite as "the known level" is a mirror
measurement.

With `--opponent`, at 60 games per condition:

```text
mirror                 0.66%    (reproduces the historical figure: the harness validates)
crustle_wall_9         4.93%
alakazam_1             5.32%
marnie_grimmsnarl_1    6.18%
```

**Between seven and nine times more**, consistent across three archetypes.

### What B9 corrected in that reading

B9 broke the excess down at 300 games per condition, and **forks between TYPES
of play do not multiply**:

| | order-dependent | between types | fork rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| mirror | 0.64% | 70 | **0.18%** |
| `crustle_wall_9` | 4.62% | 26 | **0.06%** |
| `alakazam_1` | 5.02% | 101 | **0.24%** |
| `marnie_grimmsnarl_1` | 5.98% | 44 | **0.11%** |

The mirror sits **in the middle**. So "attack-or-retreat is decided by the menu"
does not get worse against real decks.

**But it is not cosmetic either, and the inherited reading needs correcting
here.** The earlier plans take for granted that "a `CARD` vs `CARD` tie is
cosmetic", and the probe itself disproves that: `meaning()` resolves each option
down to the CARD, so two copies of the same card give the same description and
**do not count as a divergence**. A `CARD` vs `CARD` tie is two **different**
cards.

What actually explodes against real lists is the same type with a different
object:

```text
crustle_wall_9    PLAY vs PLAY 663   ATTACH vs ATTACH 645   CARD vs CARD 449+89
alakazam_1        CARD 1004+150+137  ATTACH 241   PLAY 192   EVOLVE 143
marnie_grim._1    CARD 1296+246+119  PLAY 139     ABILITY 86
```

`ATTACH:1->710` against `ATTACH:1->150` is charging **a different body**;
`PLAY:X` against `PLAY:Y` is benching **a different Pokémon**. Those are
different plays, not copies of the same one.

**The honest conclusion:** against a real deck, one decision in twenty is
decided by the menu's order, and what gets decided that way is **which card,
which body or which target** — not which CLASS of play it is. Less alarming than
the raw headline and considerably more than "cosmetic".

What pulled the thread was triaging the 73 `ATTACK` vs `RETREAT` forks: **67 of
the 73 have our active at full health and the bench full** — this is not a
wounded body deciding whether to flee — and the "opposing" actives were
Hydrapple ex, Tapu Bulu, Applin: our own cards.

### 3.2 The monitor spent 2.1 GB recording that everything was fine

B3 dumped **196 575 files**, all `STALE_FLAG` or `STALE_READ`, which the
monitor's own docstring documents as non-defects. The cost is not the disk: it
is that on the night a real violation lands, it will land as one file among two
hundred thousand that mean nothing. `--dump` now writes only the categories that
are defects, and **says out loud how many it left out**.

### 3.3 The radar's signal is confounded with game length

B6's four candidates are all in `juega_supporter`:

```text
mega_kangaskhan_4   16.4%      otro_bramblin_1   30.2%
otro_comfey_1       28.3%      chandelure_1      32.8%      (median 58.4%)
```

But across the 87 lists, **game length against Supporter resolution gives
r = −0.55**, and the three decks with the longest games are three of the four
candidates. `mega_kangaskhan_4` is extreme on both axes: 16 260 productive
turns, **three times any other**, roughly 40 turns per game against the typical
~12.

It is not entirely ruled out — `crustle_wall_2` is the fourth longest with a
normal 60.1% resolution, so length does not force it — but **the signal cannot
be read as a collision without controlling for length**. The question that does
stay alive, and is cheap: why do games against `mega_kangaskhan_4` last three
times as long?
